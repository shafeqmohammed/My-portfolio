Beyond the Interface: What Product Design Really Solves

A beautiful interface can still be a bad product. The real test is whether people understand what to do, know what happens next and can recover when something goes wrong.

The question

We spend a great deal of time refining colour, typography, spacing and visual consistency. These things matter. But they don’t automatically make a product easier to use. So what is product design actually for?

Why it matters

When teams judge design by how it looks, they optimise the wrong thing. The people using a complex product — an operator watching a fleet, an engineer configuring infrastructure, an administrator granting access — are not looking at the interface. They are trying to get something done, often under time pressure, with real consequences if they get it wrong.

The principles

Good product design answers four questions at every step:

  • What is happening right now? The system communicates its state.
  • What requires my attention? Hierarchy reflects the user’s priorities, not the database’s.
  • What happens if I take this action? Consequences are visible before commitment.
  • What should I do when something fails? Errors explain how to recover, not just what went wrong.

None of this is new. “Visibility of system status” has been one of Jakob Nielsen’s usability heuristics for three decades. What has changed is the complexity of the systems we design for.

An example

Hypothetical scenario. A dashboard shows hundreds of data points about a fleet of connected devices. Every chart is well designed. But when a device goes offline, the operator has to scan six panels to find it, can’t tell whether it is a brief dip or a real outage, and has no indication of what to do next.

That is a good visualisation, not a good experience. The improvement is not more polish. It is a clear state vocabulary, alerts ranked by impact, and a single obvious next action.

Good product design is about reducing uncertainty — and helping people take the right action with confidence.

My perspective

I think product design solves uncertainty. Visual craft earns attention and trust; it is necessary. But the value of design is measured in decisions people can make, tasks they can complete and mistakes they can recover from.

What to do

  • Start reviews with the decisions users need to make, not with the visuals.
  • Define every system state and the next action for each.
  • Write error messages that say how to fix the problem.
  • Validate with real tasks — usability sessions, telemetry or support trends — not opinions.

Limits and trade-offs

For brand-led and emotional products, visual expression is part of the job, not decoration. And in early exploration, polish can help stakeholders imagine what is possible. The argument here is about priority, not about craft being unimportant.
